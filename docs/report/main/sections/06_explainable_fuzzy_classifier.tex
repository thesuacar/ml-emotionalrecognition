
\section{Task 4: Explainable fuzzy classifier}
\label{sec:fuzzy}

\textbf{Features and protocol.}
We extracted seven geometric features from the 68 dlib facial
landmarks: mouth width, mouth opening, left and right eye opening,
eyebrow height, mouth width-to-height ratio (\emph{smile ratio}),
and mouth-corner asymmetry. Most distances were normalized by face
width, while eye opening was normalized by eye width. All 980
frontal KDEF images were processed successfully, without landmark
detection failures.

The predefined subject-independent split contained 56 training
subjects (784 images) and 14 held-out test subjects (196 images).
Within the training partition, 44 subjects (616 images) were used
for fitting and 12 (168 images) for validation. MinMax scaling
was fitted only on the corresponding training partition.

\textbf{Fuzzy model.}
We trained seven one-versus-rest Takagi-Sugeno fuzzy models with
PyFUME, one for each emotion. The standard settings were modified, which changes PyFUME's default rule simplification and holdout. The relevant parameters altered are: $setnes\_threshold=1.0$ , $merge\_threshold=1.0$ and $data\_split\_method$ $=$ $"no\_split"$. 

Each model uses Gaussian membership
functions in its rule antecedents and a learned linear consequent.
The numerical outputs are combined through fuzzy inference, and
the emotion with the highest output score is selected. These
scores are not calibrated probabilities.

We compared two and three clusters per emotion using macro-F1
on validation subjects. The three-cluster configuration was
selected before testing and subsequently refitted on all 784
training images.

\textbf{Rule interpretation.}
The final system contains 21 fuzzy rules, three per emotion.
A representative rule from the happiness model is:

\begin{quote}
\small
\emph{Happy, Rule 1 (abridged):}
IF mouth width belongs to cluster 1 (Gaussian centre 0.762)
AND mouth opening belongs to cluster 1 (centre 0.204),
together with the remaining geometric conditions,
THEN calculate the happiness score using the fitted linear
consequent.
\end{quote}

This rule dominated 94.6\% of happy training images but only
2.2\% of other images. Its relatively wide-mouth configuration
is consistent with smiling. The strongest rules for fear and
sadness showed weaker separation, and overlapping membership
functions limit their linguistic interpretation.

\textbf{Evaluation and limitations.}
On unseen subjects, happiness (28/28), surprise (26/28) and
disgust (23/28) achieved the highest recall. Fear (7/28) and
sadness (8/28) were more difficult: 14 fearful faces were
classified as surprise, and 12 sad faces as neutral.
The roughly ten-percentage-point accuracy decrease from
validation to testing indicates limited generalization across
individuals. Although the fuzzy antecedents are inspectable,
the linear consequents and interactions between the seven
models complicate explanations of individual predictions.

For comparison, Table~\ref{tab:kdef} reports the Task 2 classifiers
trained on HOG features and evaluated on the same 14 held-out KDEF
subjects (196 images). The fuzzy model reaches a macro-F1 of 0.608,
slightly above the SVM selected on validation (0.604) and below the
Random Forest (0.614), which scored best on the test subjects but was
not selected on validation. Its accuracy of 63.27\% is the highest of
the three (SVM 60.20\%, Random Forest 62.24\%, Logistic Regression
57.65\%). These gaps correspond to a few images out of 196, so the
fuzzy model performs comparably while using seven interpretable
measurements instead of 900 HOG features. Its drop from validation
(0.709) to test (0.608) is larger than the SVM's (0.679 to 0.604),
which is consistent with the limited generalization noted above.
